What the numbers do — and don't — say

Every figure below is from a Hong Kong source and includes its year, sample, measure, and denominator. A number without context is misleading — so we always show the frame. This page is separate from Jay's fictional story on purpose.

Key principle: A screening score is not a diagnosis. A population estimate cannot describe any individual. We name each measure exactly and do not upgrade it into a clinical claim.

It's been getting worse, not better

A 5.5-year study tracking 11 cohorts of Hong Kong university students (from Semester 2 of 2015/16 to Semester 2 of 2020/21) found a statistically significant rising trend across every negative mental health indicator:

  • Psychological distress
  • Perceived stress
  • Depressive symptoms
  • Negative thoughts and negative emotions

The effect sizes were medium (η² = 0.045–0.103), meaning the change was meaningful, not just noise. By the end of the study period, 12.8% of students reported severe depressive symptoms and 14.1% showed high psychological distress.

Note: This study spans the COVID-19 pandemic. Rising trends may reflect pandemic-related stress as well as longer-term pressures. We cannot separate the two from this data alone.

What we can say with confidence

1. Stress among Hong Kong university students is common

Multiple independent studies — a 5.5-year cohort study and a 2025 territory-wide survey — both find that a large share of students report symptoms of stress, anxiety, or depression. This is not a fringe experience.

2. Young adults (18–24) are the most affected age group

The 2025 CUHK survey found that 18–24-year-olds had higher depression and anxiety scores than any other adult age group, with 43.5% reporting moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms.

3. Most people don't seek help

55% of 2025 survey respondents said they would not or were unsure about seeking professional help. The most common reasons were "too busy" and "I believe I can cope on my own." The gap between needing support and getting it is one of the biggest problems we face.

4. Screen time and stress are linked

The 2025 survey found a significant positive correlation between screen time and depression/anxiety scores among young adults. This is an association, not proof of causation — but it's consistent with sleep and attention research.

5. Even light movement helps

The same survey confirmed a strong positive relationship between casual movement (walking, stretching) and mental health — the key is "doing something," not intensity.

What we cannot say — honest uncertainty

We cannot diagnose Jay or anyone else. A story and a set of statistics cannot tell us whether a specific person has a condition.

We cannot say these are all "depression" figures. Most numbers come from screening tools (BDI, GHQ-12, PHQ-9), which measure symptoms, not clinical diagnoses. The actual rate of diagnosed depression in Hong Kong is much lower — 1.2% in the Department of Health's Population Health Survey 2020–22 — because many people with symptoms never see a clinician. That is the most recent official figure, and it shows how differently screening and diagnosis measure the same population.

We cannot say screen time causes stress. The data shows a correlation, but stressed students may also use screens more as a coping mechanism. The direction of cause is unclear.

We cannot say every stressed student needs clinical help. Stress is a normal part of university life. The question is whether it's persistent and impairing — which only a qualified professional can assess.

We cannot guarantee current prevalence. The 5.5-year study ends in 2021; the 2025 survey is the most recent but uses a different method. True current prevalence may differ.

How to read these numbers responsibly

Check the denominator

"68.5%" of what? Of 960 local university students in one study, measured by BDI-II screening. Without the who and the how, the percentage is incomplete.

Check the measure

Was it a screening questionnaire or a clinical diagnosis? Screening finds many more people; diagnosis confirms fewer. Both are useful — for different questions.

Check the date

The 5.5-year study ends in 2021. The CUHK survey is from 2025. Mental health patterns shift; always look for the most recent verified source.

Check the leap

Does the claim use the measure's exact name, or does it turn "depressive symptoms" into "depression"? The safe reading is usually the narrower one.

Numbers are one part of the picture

Statistics show the scale, but they don't describe a day. Jay's story offers the human side. And if something here feels familiar, the support page lists verified Hong Kong resources.
